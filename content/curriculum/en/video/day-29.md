Until now every pixel in your videos came from code you could read. Today you add footage nobody filmed: clips
generated by a video model through an API. Used well, it's the B-roll you could never afford. Used badly, it's three
clips that look like three different films and a surprise bill.

## What video models are (and aren't) good at

You send text (and optionally an image), wait from 30 seconds to a few minutes, and get back a short clip, usually
4–10 seconds long. The main families in late 2026: **Google Veo** (via the Gemini API), **Runway**, **Luma**, **Kling**,
and open-weight models like **Wan**. Aggregators such as **Replicate** and **fal** give you one key and one bill for
dozens of them. Names change every few months: check the provider's current model list.

**Good at:** atmosphere, nature, slow camera moves, abstract textures, establishing shots, product-on-table moods.
**Bad at:** readable text, hands doing precise things, the same face across clips, exact counts ("five birds"), anything
that must be factually right. Rule of thumb: let AI do the *mood*, let your code do the *meaning* (titles, numbers,
diagrams, captions).

**A video prompt is a shot description**, not a wish. Use this order:

1. **Subject + action:** "a paper boat drifting down a rain-filled gutter"
2. **Camera:** shot size (close-up, wide), movement (slow dolly in, static tripod, low tracking shot, drone pull-back)
3. **Light + lens:** "overcast morning, shallow depth of field, 35mm"
4. **Style:** "muted teal and amber palette, soft film grain"
5. **Exclusions:** "no text, no logos, no people"

**Consistency** comes from three tricks: a fixed *style suffix* pasted onto every prompt, a *reference image* (most APIs
accept a starting frame — image-to-video), and the same aspect ratio and duration for every clip. Some APIs also
take a seed.

**Cost is per second of output**, and you'll throw away 1–2 attempts for every keeper. Estimate before you run:
`clips × seconds × price per second × 2.5`. Read the pricing page, put the number in your script, and set a spend
limit in the provider's dashboard first.

**Rights and disclosure.** Read the provider's terms: who owns the output, whether commercial use is allowed on your
plan. Never generate real people, brands or copyrighted characters. YouTube, TikTok and Instagram ask you to label
realistic synthetic footage, and some providers embed invisible watermarks (Google's SynthID, for example). Disclose
anyway, in the description.

## Step by step

> **A new home for your tools.** Days 15–27 each had a `dayNN/` folder. From today the pieces grow into one project:
> create `~/66/video/pipeline/` and work there — every path from now on is relative to it. Gitignore `clips/`, `out/`,
> `runs/` and `.env`. Remotion stays in `~/66/video/remotion/`; old `dayNN/` scripts stay put, and the agent reads or
> copies them when a prompt names them.

### 1. Pick three shots from your day-27 explainer

Open your explainer's script and choose three moments that would feel richer with real-looking footage: an opening
mood shot, a metaphor, a closing image. Write one line per shot. Generate them in 16:9 even though day 27 was vertical:
a horizontal master can be cropped to 9:16 later (day 34), never the other way round. Generate (or draw in code) one
**reference still** for the look: a single frame with the palette and light you want.

### 2. Build the generator

```text
Goal: a Python script gen_clips.py that turns shots.json into 3 video clips through a video-generation API.
Context: my pipeline/ folder (~/66/video/pipeline). API key is in .env as VIDEO_API_KEY (provider: <Gemini API Veo | Replicate | fal>).
Check the provider's current docs for the model name and the async "start job → poll → download" flow.
Constraints:
- shots.json has: id, prompt, duration_s, and a shared "style_suffix" and "reference_image".
- Every prompt = shot prompt + style_suffix. Same aspect ratio (16:9) and duration for all.
- Before calling the API, print estimated cost (price per second in a constant I'll fill in) and ask y/N.
- --dry-run prints the final prompts and cost only. --fixture copies clips from fixtures/ instead of calling the API.
- Never print the key. Save to clips/<id>_v<N>.mp4, never overwrite; append every attempt to prompt_log.jsonl.
Done when: python gen_clips.py --dry-run shows 3 prompts and a cost, and a real run produces 3 mp4 files.
```

Run `--dry-run` first: would a cinematographer know what to shoot from these prompts?

> **If you're stuck:** jobs that "never finish" are usually a polling bug. Ask the agent to print the raw status
> response on every poll. A "safety" rejection usually means a word in the prompt sounds like a real person or brand —
> rephrase it.

> **No key or budget?** Use `--fixture` with three free stock clips (Pexels or Pixabay videos, check each licence).
> Everything else works the same; swap in AI clips later.

### 3. Change one thing at a time

Re-run one shot twice, each time changing **one** element: the camera move, then the light. Stitch the three keepers
side by side to judge consistency:

```bash
ffmpeg -i clips/s1_v2.mp4 -i clips/s2_v1.mp4 -i clips/s3_v3.mp4 \
  -filter_complex "[0:v]scale=640:-2[a];[1:v]scale=640:-2[b];[2:v]scale=640:-2[c];[a][b][c]hstack=3" \
  -t 5 compare.mp4
```

Upload the three keepers (or `compare.mp4`). For the prompt log, paste 3–5 lines like: *"changed 'handheld' to
'static tripod' → jitter gone, felt calmer; added 'overcast' → palette finally matched shot 1."*

## Quiz

1. You need 3 clips × 6 s, the price is P per second, and you expect 2 rejected attempts per keeper. What's your estimate?
2. Shot 2 has different colours from shots 1 and 3. Name two fixes.
3. Can you put a generated clip in a paid client ad?
4. Which part of a "how compound interest works" video should *not* be AI-generated?

> **Answers:**
>
> 1. 3 clips × 3 attempts × 6 s × P = 54 × P. Write that number down before you press Enter.
> 2. Reuse the exact style suffix and reference image; mention the palette and light explicitly; regenerate only that shot.
> 3. Only if the provider's terms on your plan allow commercial use, the clip shows no real people, brands or protected
>    characters, and the client knows it's AI footage.
> 4. The numbers, formulas and charts — anything that must be correct. Render those in code.
