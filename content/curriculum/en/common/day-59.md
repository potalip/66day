You can build things people use and make videos people watch. Somebody out there is looking for exactly that and
has budget for it. Today you stop guessing where they are and look at real requests with real prices.

## Five places paid work lives

- **Upwork** — the biggest marketplace. Competitive, and sending proposals costs "Connects" (check current
  pricing). Filter by *Entry level* and *Fixed price*, posted in the last 3 days, fewer than 10 proposals.
- **Contra** — portfolio-first, popular with creators and startups; you can also sell fixed packages. Check its
  current fee terms.
- **Local businesses** — a café, a dentist, a gym, a small shop. They rarely post jobs, but they have real
  problems: booking, reminders, menus, promo videos for Reels. One walk-in or a polite DM can beat 30 proposals.
- **Creators** — YouTubers, podcasters and newsletter writers need shorts cut from long videos, thumbnails,
  captions, small tools for their audience. Look at who you already follow.
- **Cold outreach** — a short, specific email to a business where you spotted a problem: "Your booking form fails
  on mobile; here's a 40-second video of a fix I built for a similar studio."

Pick a **niche** — what you do for whom. "Telegram booking bots for beauty studios" or "60-second product
explainers for indie SaaS" wins more work than "I do apps and videos". Narrow feels scary; it's what makes you
memorable.

## Honest pricing for beginners

Price the **deliverable**, not your hours: "a booking bot with reminders, deployed, 1 revision round, 5 days".
Then sanity-check it:

1. Look at what similar postings offer (you'll collect 10 today). Start in the lower-middle of that range — not
   the bottom. Racing to the cheapest price attracts the worst clients.
2. Estimate your hours honestly, then add 50%: client communication and surprises are real work.
3. Divide price by those hours. If the hourly number would upset you, raise the price or shrink the scope.
4. Write what's **not** included: hosting fees, AI usage, extra revisions. Set up AI keys and hosting on the
   *client's* accounts so they own them and pay usage directly.

Your first 2–3 projects can be priced as "portfolio rate" — lower, but never free. Raise prices after three good
reviews.

Two honest notes. First, expect weeks rather than days before the first paid job — and many beginners land it
through someone they already know, not a marketplace. Second, before the first payment arrives, check how freelance
income is registered and taxed where you live, and which ways of receiving money from abroad are legally open to you.

## Step 1 — Ten real postings (35 min)

Browse by hand (don't scrape platforms — it breaks their terms). Collect 10 requests you could deliver in under a
week, in a table: link, what they want, their budget, your estimate in hours, your price. Mix sources: at least
two that aren't marketplaces. Then ask your agent to check your thinking:

```text
Here are 10 job postings I collected, with my estimates: (paste the table).
For each: what's the hidden work they didn't mention, is my estimate realistic for a beginner who
builds with an AI coding agent, and which 3 are the best fit for my portfolio (link)? Be blunt.
Don't rewrite the table — add one comment line under each row.
```

## Step 2 — Your offer (20 min)

One sentence, one price, one deliverable:

```text
I build Telegram booking bots for beauty studios — reminders, cancellations and a weekly summary —
deployed in 5 days for a fixed price, one revision included.
```

```text
I turn one long podcast episode into 5 captioned vertical shorts with your branding,
delivered in 3 days, for a fixed price per episode.
```

Add your price under it. If you can't say it out loud without wincing, it's either too low or the scope is too big.

## Quiz: red-flag clients

1. "Before we hire you, build the full app as a free test." Red flag or normal?
2. A client offers to pay *more* than your price, outside the platform, and asks you to send back the difference.
3. "It's a small change" — for the fourth time, on a fixed-price job.
4. A posting wants "an Uber clone with AI" for a budget smaller than a nice dinner, delivered by tomorrow.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Red flag. A short paid test or a 15-minute sample is normal; a full free build is free labour.
2. Classic overpayment scam. Never send money back; keep payments on the platform or through a proper invoice.
3. Scope creep. Kindly point to the agreed scope and offer a price for the extra work. A written scope protects
   both of you.
4. Unrealistic scope, budget and deadline at once. Skip it, or reply with what *is* possible for that budget.

</details>
